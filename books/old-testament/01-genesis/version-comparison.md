**Where each version departs from the Hebrew**

- **KJV** is furthest from the text on three points: singular "heaven" (the Hebrew has no singular), "moved" for a verb that means hovering or fluttering, and "the first day" for "day one." "Divided" for *vayyavdel* is acceptable. Its "without form, and void" is a fair pair for *tohu va-vohu*.
- **NIV / ESV** fix "heavens" and "hovering" but keep "the first day" and "In the beginning" with the article.
- **NASB** is the closest of the Christian versions: "the heavens," "hovering" (2020), and "one day" for *yom echad*. It still supplies the article in 1:1.
- **NRSV / JPS** are the only ones that take the grammar of *bereshit* seriously (no article → construct clause, with v.2 as background and v.3 as the first main action). Both render *ruach* as "wind," which is lexically defensible but ignores that the verb and parallel uses point to an agent. JPS "a first day" is a careful compromise; NRSV reverts to "the first day."
- **No major version** keeps the doubled "between" in 1:4 or the two-word symmetry of 1:3 ("Let light be / light was"); all smooth these into English idiom.

**Net:** NASB agrees with this log on the most points among Christian Bibles; JPS agrees on the most grammatically significant point (the structure of vv.1–3). KJV, the version most readers hold as the standard, is the least literal of the six in these verses, though every one of its departures is a matter of English style rather than a different Hebrew text.

Every version renders YHWH as "the LORD" in small capitals, following the Jewish practice of saying *Adonai*; none prints the name. On *ed*, KJV, ESV and NASB keep "mist" from the Targum, while NIV, NRSV and JPS follow the Hebrew's upward-moving water. On *nefesh chayyah*, every version renders 2:7 differently from 1:20–30; KJV's "soul" is the sharpest split.

On *shavat*, only JPS keeps "ceased"; every Christian version has "rested," which is the word for a different Hebrew verb (*nuach*, Ex 20:11). NASB 2020 narrows *tzava* to "heavenly lights," deciding for the reader that the host is the stars; the Hebrew word covers both stars and beings and the verse applies it to the earth too. All six follow the MT "seventh" in 2:2 against the LXX, Samaritan and Syriac "sixth."

All six versions keep "let us make" and "male and female." On *adam*, the versions split between "man" (KJV, ESV, JPS) and a collective ("mankind," "humankind"); the plural pronouns in vv.26–28 are in every version, so the collective sense is visible in all of them, but "man" lets an English reader hear a male individual. On *radah*, "have dominion" and "rule" are both accurate; KJV's phrase became the technical term in later debates. The sixth day is the one place in the chapter where KJV's "the" is in the Hebrew.

KJV's "whales" comes from the Greek *kētē* through Latin *cete*; it turns the Hebrew chaos-monster word into a species. KJV's 1:20 syntax ("Let the waters bring forth… and fowl") makes the birds a product of the water, which the Hebrew does not say; ESV, NASB, NRSV and JPS all restore the separate command. On *nefesh chayyah*, KJV renders one Hebrew phrase four ways in five verses (1:20, 21, 24, 30; 2:7).

Only two versions render *mo'adim* as the calendar word it is: NIV 2011 "sacred times" and JPS "set times." KJV "seasons" was accurate in 1611 (a season was an appointed time) and has since drifted to mean spring, summer, autumn, winter. Every version turns the plain adjectives "great/small" into comparatives; a small smoothing, but it hides that the text avoids ranking the lamps against anything but each other.

KJV's "grass" for *deshe* and "herb" for *esev* are 17th-century English for sprouting growth and plants generally; "grass" now reads too narrowly. KJV "his kind" uses the old neuter possessive, not a masculine one. NRSV/JPS "of every kind" shifts *min* from reproduction ("to its kind") to variety, which the Hebrew phrase does not say.
